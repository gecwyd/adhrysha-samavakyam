# Same Four Years, Different Clocks

Create exactly FIVE scenes, each EIGHT seconds long, for a 40-second English animated editorial film FOR ADULTS, not a children's educational cartoon. Landscape 16:9, 1080p, with intelligible English dialogue/narration. Each video prompt must explicitly request an eight-second animated video with audio, 16:9 composition. No title cards or rendered subtitles; captions will be added separately on the website. No logos or watermarks. No readable text or numerals anywhere in the frame: clock faces use tick marks only.

Style anchor to repeat in every prompt: sophisticated hand-drawn 2D graphic-novel animation for an adult literary magazine; warm vintage paper #d9d4c7, confident charcoal ink #191713, restrained burnt orange #c86632 and muted teal #597b79; fine crosshatching, subtle grain, cinematic shadows, realistic adult proportions, deliberate composition and understated movement. Think an illustrated science essay, not a children's show. No chibi figures, oversized eyes or heads, cute rockets, bouncing motion, slapstick, floating emoji, memory bubbles, candy colors, toy-like props, exaggerated reactions, or cheerful nursery music. Mature means thoughtful and visually restrained, not violent or hypermasculine.

The cartoon clock reference is NOT the visual style for this film. Do not depict an anthropomorphic clock or a mascot inside the film; Miss Minute will appear separately in the magazine's essay summary. Two original Indian graduating students in their early twenties: Anu wears a muted teal shirt, has medium-brown skin and a short dark bob; Dev wears a dark rust shirt, has medium-brown skin and short curly black hair. Both have natural adult proportions and understated expressions. Campus setting suggests the green hills of Wayanad. Voices remain consistent: Anu reflective, Dev quietly candid, an off-screen narrator composed and conversational. No imitation of a particular actor. Use sparse atmospheric sound and very quiet ambient music; narration must dominate. Let the imagery carry the explanation instead of a presenter performing it.

Continuity: each scene begins at the previous scene's end, using drawn transitions and camera movement. Follow the supplied concise dialogue verbatim, with natural pauses. Do not add dialogue.

## Design language (applies to every scene)

**The idea in one line:** the whole film is a page from Anu's notebook, drawn by an unseen pen. Scenes 2–4 are what she sketches while Dev talks; scenes 1 and 5 are the life around the margins.

**One orange thread.** A single hair-thin burnt-orange line runs through the film. It starts as the sweep of a wristwatch's second hand, becomes the spacecraft's trajectory, drops as a plumb line beside the tower, and ends as the path the two students walk. Burnt orange appears ONLY on this thread and in Dev's rust shirt; nothing else glows orange.

**Colour roles.** Teal belongs to Anu, to Earth, and to the stay-at-home clock. Charcoal ink carries every form, shadow and crosshatch. Paper stays visible in every frame; the drawing never fills to the edge, and negative space is treated as a design element, not leftover.

**Line quality.** Ink lines are drawn on as the camera watches: contour first, then crosshatch, then a wash of muted colour. Motion is slow and deliberate; nothing bounces or snaps. Occasional paper-fibre drift and pencil construction lines remain visible under the ink.

**Sound motif.** One quiet, dry wristwatch tick is the only rhythmic sound in the film. Its spacing is the story: even in scene 1, stretched and lonely in the exam hall of scene 2, split into two slightly different tempos in scene 3, lower and heavier in scene 4, and finally dissolved into soft rain in scene 5. Beneath it, a single sustained low string or piano note; no melody, no swell. Narration always sits on top.

**Camera.** Slow lateral tracking and gentle push-ins, as if the camera were a pen moving across the page. No whip pans, no shaky-cam, no zoom bursts.

## Scene 1 — The same four years (8 seconds)
Late afternoon after the last exam. A quiet hill-campus corridor of worn concrete, rain-stained pillars and a row of empty notice boards; ID lanyards and a jacket hang on a hook. Anu and Dev walk toward the gate carrying notebooks, long shadows stretching ahead of them, the misty green Wayanad hills waiting beyond the gate. Warm low light rakes across the floor. Brief close-ups: Anu's soft half-smile, Dev's tired, honest glance sideways. No comic performance.

Beats: 0–1s establishing wide with ambient birdsong and distant wind; 1–3s Anu's line; 3–5s Dev's line; 5–7s narrator over the two of them from behind, the gate and hills ahead; 7–8s push in to Dev's wrist, an ordinary wristwatch, its second hand tracing a thin burnt-orange line as it moves.

Anu: "Four years! They flew by."
Dev: "Really? Mine felt endless."
Narrator: "Same years. Different feelings."
End on a close-up of the wristwatch; match-cut its circular face into the next composition.

## Scene 2 — Feeling versus measuring (8 seconds)
The watch face becomes the centre of a split page. Left panel: the two friends in a loose, warm conversation on a corridor ledge, while the clock behind them is drawn with quick, blurred hand movement. Right panel: a student alone in a silent examination hall, rows of empty desks and one lit window, the identical clock on the wall drawn with long, patient strokes. Both clocks are the same size and register the same interval; only the drawing of them changes, a visual argument that perceived time is not physical time dilation. Then the architecture's lines lift off the page, unspool into fine perspective lines, and become a technical space illustration on paper.

Beats: 0–2s panel one with the tick even; 2–4s panel two with the tick stretched and hollow; 4–6s narrator as the room's lines dissolve into drafting lines; 6–8s two precise clock diagrams settle side by side.

Narrator: "That is how time feels. But in physics, moving clocks can actually record less time."
End on two precise clock diagrams side by side, one beside Earth and one inside a realistically proportioned spacecraft.

## Scene 3 — A journey near light speed (8 seconds)
A spare orbital diagram in ink-dark space: a small teal Earth low in the frame with a clock resting beside it, and a slender, realistically proportioned spacecraft beginning its trip. The orange thread leaves Earth as the trajectory line, arcs far out, and the craft turns around and comes home; light speed is suggested only by the length and thinness of the line, never by streaks or effects, and never faster than light. The camera tracks slowly along the path, then settles. Both clocks tick normally in their own frame (the two tick sounds drift out of step), but the travelling clock's hand has turned less. Use comparative clock-hand movement, not numbers and not comical ageing effects.

Beats: 0–2s the departure; 2–4s the far turn-around; 4–6s the return along the line; 6–8s reunion, the two clocks framed together, the Earth clock's hand visibly further round.

Narrator: "Travel close to light speed and return. Your clock records less time than the clock left on Earth."
End with both clocks reunited, hands showing different amounts of elapsed time.

## Scene 4 — Gravity changes clocks too (8 seconds)
The reunited clocks match-cut into an architectural section of a tall tower, drawn in fine drafting lines with generous negative space, as if the pen were a draughtsman's. The orange thread drops from the top as a plumb line. One clock sits at the tower's base near Earth's surface, another high at the summit; a clean comparison inset shows the difference, exaggerated purely for explanation, and the drawing makes no claim that everyday gravity produces a large gap. Keep this visually distinct from the spacecraft example: vertical and architectural rather than orbital. The camera then draws back until the tower is revealed to be a sketch on a page of Anu's notebook, resting on a campus bench, a pen and a folded sleeve at its edge.

Beats: 0–2s tower section is drawn on; 2–5s base and summit clocks, inset; 5–8s slow pull back to the notebook on the bench in soft, dimming light.

Narrator: "Gravity matters too. A clock lower down, in stronger gravity, ticks slightly slower than one higher up."
End by pulling back from the architectural drawing to a notebook on a campus bench.

## Scene 5 — What the clock cannot tell (8 seconds)
Back on campus at dusk. Anu closes the notebook beside Dev; neither speaks. The tick softens into rain. Quiet editorial match cuts, each anchored by a shape from the last: the notebook's rectangle becomes a rain-streaked corridor window; the bench's line becomes an empty bench under a tin roof; a hand lifted in a lingering farewell becomes two figures at the gate. Use grounded cinematic imagery, not literal memory clouds. This is explicitly a poetic transition, not experimental evidence for relativity.

Beats: 0–2s notebook closes, rain begins; 2–4s corridor and empty bench cuts; 4–6s narrator over the farewell at the gate; 6–8s a wide architectural composition, the two figures small in it, the orange thread now the path they walk, continuing softly into the paper.

Narrator: "Physics measures the difference between clocks. Memories tell another story: not how long we lived, but how deeply."
End on the two small adult figures walking away through a wide architectural composition, with ample warm paper negative space for the magazine's transition into an essay.
